# Wire it

Every wire in the build, one at a time. Tick each off as you make it; the ticks stay in this browser.

<WireIt />

Find a pin by the **name printed beside it** on your board, not by where it sits in the
drawing. The order of every board's pins was read off photos of your own parts.
Wire colours are a suggestion.

Node A is the **ESP8266 NodeMCU** the lab handed over, not the ESP32 DevKit on the
request. The firmware was moved to it on 23 October 2026; the pins below are its pins.

## Before power goes on

<Callout type="danger" title="Three NodeMCU pins have a job at power-on">

**D3** (GPIO 0) and **D4** (GPIO 2) must not be held low while the board starts, and
**D8** (GPIO 15) must not be held high. Wired as drawn, all three are safe: D3 is the
capture button, so do not hold it while plugging in, and D8 drives the transistor. Do
not move anything else onto them. A board held wrong at power-on looks dead, or sits
waiting to be flashed.

</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="Your NodeMCU may be too wide for the breadboard">

Boards of this layout (CH340, "LoLin V3") are wider than most: across the breadboard's
middle, their pins land on the outermost holes and the board covers every hole beside
them. If yours does, leave it **off** the breadboard and run **female–male jumpers** from
its pins to the breadboard. Node A needs ten (3V, G, VU, D1, D2, D3, D5, D6, D7, D8),
and you have ten.

</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="The camera's pins are inside its programmer base">

On its MB base, none of the ESP32-CAM's pins can be reached, and the light needs IO13.
Flash it and set its Wi-Fi on the base first. Then note which way round it sits, lift it
off, and put it across the breadboard's middle. Four **male–male jumpers** from the base's
now-empty sockets (5V, GND, U0R, U0T) to the camera's pins of the same names, and the base
still powers it over USB and the bench page still reads it. To flash it again, put it back
on the base. Not yet tried on your parts; if the camera resets during a photo, power is
the suspect, and the 100 µF across its 5V and GND is the first fix.

</Callout>

<Callout type="danger" title="Nothing that idles high on the camera's GPIO 12">

The ESP32 reads GPIO 12 at boot to pick the flash voltage. Held high, the board does not
start, and it looks like a dead board. The light is on GPIO 13 for this reason.

</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="Find the transistor's legs before you plug it in">

Yours read **2N2222A**. Flat face towards you, legs down, the base is the middle leg, and
the usual order is emitter, base, collector. Some makers swap the outer two, so check
once: a multimeter on diode test, red probe on the middle leg, black on each outer leg.
Both read about 0.6 to 0.7 V; the slightly higher one is the emitter.

</Callout>

First power-up: the bench supply at 5.00 V with a 1 A current limit, so a wiring mistake
costs nothing.

## Power

A brownout mid-capture is a corrupt photo and a lost weight.

- **5 V, 2 A or more**, into the camera's 5V pin. Not 1 A.
- **A 1000 µF** across it, as close to the board as it will go (the board has its own small ceramics).
- **Short, thick leads. No breadboard** in the camera's supply.
- **One lead from the supply to each board**, joined only at the supply, with the second 1000 µF there.
- **The HX711 on Node A's 3V3**, never on the camera's supply.

## HX711 settings

| Setting | Value | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Channel | A | The only one with gain 128 |
| Gain | 128 | Matches the load cell's output |
| Rate | 10 samples a second | 80 is far noisier, and the plate is not moving |
| Averaging | Median of 10 to 16 | A median rejects a single wild sample; a mean does not |

What this buys is on [Accuracy](/reference/accuracy).
